How to floss properly

Five simple steps, the mistakes that make flossing hurt, what to use if floss is not for you, and a few good videos so you can see it done.

From the team at Bite Dental Clinic, West Delhi and Gurugram · Updated October 2026

Share on WhatsApp
The short version: clean between your teeth once a day. Slide the floss in gently, hug each tooth in a C-shape, and move it up and down under the gumline. Use a fresh section for every gap. If floss is hard for you, interdental brushes are a good alternative. The best method is the one you will actually do every day.

Why bother flossing?

Your toothbrush cleans the front, back and top of each tooth. It cannot reach the narrow gaps between teeth, which is where plaque builds up unnoticed. That plaque is a common starting point for cavities between teeth, bleeding gums and bad breath.

An honest note: studies of floss alone give mixed results, and dentists do not all agree on how much it adds on top of brushing. What most agree on is the principle: clean between your teeth every day, with whichever tool you will genuinely use. For most people that starts with floss.

How to floss in 5 steps

  1. Take enough floss

    Pull about 45 cm (18 inches), roughly forearm length. Wind most of it around the middle finger of one hand and a little around the other, leaving about 2 to 3 cm (1 inch) to work with.

  2. Hold it firmly with your thumbs and index fingers

    Keep the short section tight, like a small guitar string. This gives you control so the floss does not slip.

  3. Slide it in gently

    Use a soft back-and-forth motion to ease the floss between two teeth. Do not snap it down onto the gum.

  4. Hug the tooth in a C-shape

    Curve the floss around one tooth so it forms a C. Gently slide it just under the gumline, then move it up and down two or three times along the side of the tooth. Then curve it the other way and clean the neighbouring tooth.

  5. Move to a fresh section

    Unwind clean floss as you go so you are not putting plaque back between the next teeth. Remember the back side of your last molars, on both sides.

  • How often: once a day is enough. Before or after brushing both work. Pick a time you will stick to, such as before bed.
  • How long: about two minutes once you are practised.
  • Spit, don't rinse: after brushing and flossing, spit out the excess and avoid rinsing hard, so the fluoride stays on your teeth a little longer.

Common mistakes

  • Snapping the floss down. This can cut the gum. Ease it in with a gentle back-and-forth motion.
  • Sawing across the gumline. Hug the tooth in a C and go up and down instead.
  • Skipping the back teeth. The last molars collect plenty of plaque.
  • Reusing the same piece of floss. Use a clean section for each gap.
  • Stopping because the gums bleed. Bleeding usually means inflamed gums that need cleaning. It often settles within one to two weeks of daily flossing. If it does not, see a dentist.
  • Flossing only when food is stuck. The point is the plaque you cannot see.

Other ways to clean between teeth

Interdental brushes

Tiny brushes that suit larger gaps, braces, bridges and gum recession. Many dentists like them. Ask us which size to use, as the right one matters.

Floss picks

Easier to hold, handy for the back teeth and for beginners. Use a fresh pick when moving to another area.

Water flossers

Helpful with braces, implants and for people with limited hand movement. They can be a useful extra but should not be thought of as a complete replacement for cleaning between teeth.

Floss threaders

A small loop that helps you pass floss under braces wires and fixed bridges.

If you have braces, implants or a child

Braces and aligners. Use a floss threader or orthodontic floss to get under the wire, or interdental brushes. Aligners come out for cleaning, so floss as normal before putting them back in.

Implants, crowns and bridges. They need cleaning between them just like natural teeth. Special floss or interdental brushes are often best for bridges and implants. Ask us to show you for your own mouth.

Children. Start flossing once two teeth touch, which is often between ages 2 and 3. Children usually need an adult to do it for them until they have the dexterity, often around 8 to 10 years old. Floss picks designed for kids can make it easier. See our kids dentistry page and our guide on why baby teeth matter.

Quick questions

Should I floss before or after brushing?

Either works. Flossing first can loosen plaque so that brushing and fluoride toothpaste reach between teeth more easily, but what matters most is that you do it daily.

My gums bleed when I floss. Should I stop?

No. Bleeding usually means inflamed gums, and gentle daily cleaning helps them calm down, often within one to two weeks. If bleeding continues, or your gums are swollen or sore, book a check, as it may be gum disease. Read our bleeding gums guide.

Does flossing make gaps between teeth?

No. If gaps seem to appear after you start flossing, it is usually because swollen gums have settled back to their healthy size.

Is it okay to floss only a few times a week?

Some cleaning is better than none, but plaque rebuilds daily, so daily cleaning between teeth gives the best protection.

Is flossing painful?

It should not hurt. Mild tenderness in the first few days is common when gums are inflamed. Sharp pain can mean you are pressing too hard, or that there is a cavity, a rough filling edge or a gum problem. Let us know and we will check.

Which floss should I buy?

Waxed or unwaxed both work. Thin tape-style floss is often easier for tight contacts, and a thicker floss may suit wider gaps. If floss shreds or gets stuck, a rough edge or tight contact could be the cause, so mention it at your next visit.
